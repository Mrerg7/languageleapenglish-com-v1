Domains are only worth what a buyer will pay, and buyer willingness follows market size. The English-learning category has unusual fundamentals: the language itself is the product, the buyer base is global, and the spend keeps growing.

## The category in numbers

- Roughly **1.5 billion people** speak English worldwide, around a fifth of the global population.
- Corporate and business English training is projected to be a **multi-tens-of-billions dollar market** by the early 2030s, growing at a double-digit clip.
- Employers in dozens of countries screen for English proficiency, and professionals with strong business English report meaningfully higher earnings and faster progression.
- One in four employers reports having lost business because of a language gap.

Sources for these figures — the EF English Proficiency Index, British Council research and industry training reports — are summarised on the [market data section of this listing](/#stats).

## Why that matters for domain buyers

Three structural properties make English-learning domains unusually durable:

### 1. The demand is not a fad

Unlike crypto or a short-lived consumer trend, English education has been a growth category for decades and is reinforced by immigration, remote work and global hiring. A name bought in this category has a large, patient pool of future buyers.

### 2. The vocabulary is small and closed

There are only so many English domains that read well. Words like *language*, *english*, *speak*, *fluent* and *learn* combine into a finite set of combinations — and most obvious ones are already registered. What remains in the aftermarket is genuinely scarce.

### 3. The buyer's alternative is expensive

An edtech founder without a strong domain has three options: buy a worse name and rebrand later, spend years building recognition on an awkward URL, or pay a premium to get the right one. Only the third option has a predictable cost.

## What buyers are actually building

The category is not one market. Distinct buyers with distinct budgets compete for the same small pool of names:

- **Online English schools and platforms** launching or rebranding flagship products
- **Corporate training providers** selling into HR and L&D budgets
- **AI tutors and speech-practice apps** that need a brand explaining the promise in two words
- **Immigration, study-abroad and test-prep services** (IELTS, TOEFL, professional certification)
- **Publishers, newsletters and media** building authority around English mastery

Every one of those buyers prices a domain against marketing spend, not against registrar fees.

## Exact-match vs. invented brands

Invented names ("Duolingo", "Babbel") can work when paired with enormous marketing budgets. Exact-match names work differently: they explain themselves. A visitor seeing *languageleapenglish.com* knows the offer before reading a headline, which lowers cost per acquisition from day one and improves click-through in paid search where the keyword appears in the URL.

For a well-funded startup either path can work. For a bootstrapped team, an exact-match .com is the cheaper route to credibility.

## Risks worth naming

No investment is frictionless:

- **Trademark clearance** — run a search in your jurisdiction before adopting a name.
- **Category cyclicality** — education spend moves with corporate budgets, though far less than most sectors.
- **Execution risk** — a domain is an asset, not a business. It multiplies good execution; it does not replace it.
