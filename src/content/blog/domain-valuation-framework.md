Premium domain pricing can look arbitrary from the outside. A four-letter .com might trade for five figures while a longer, "better sounding" name sits unsold for years. In practice, serious buyers and brokers value domains using a small set of repeatable inputs.

## The six inputs that move price

### 1. Comparable sales

Nothing anchors a price like a verified recent sale of a similar name. Public datasets (NameBio, Sedo transaction reports, GoDaddy-expert sales) show what the market cleared at, not what a seller hopes for. When you compare, match on:

- **Length** in characters and in words
- **TLD** — .com trades at a premium to almost everything else
- **Semantic category** — education, finance, health and travel carry different multiples
- **Age and history** — older names with clean registration histories are easier to trust

### 2. Commercial keyword demand

A domain that contains a phrase people actively search and buy against is worth more than a name that only sounds nice. "language" and "english" are among the highest-intent terms in education: they describe the buyer's product *and* their customer's problem.

Ask whether the words in the name would appear in an ad headline, a pitch deck and a URL someone types from memory.

### 3. Length and typing accuracy

Every character adds friction. The practical ceiling is roughly 10–12 characters for a brandable name, and ideally two words or fewer. Hyphens, digits and unusual spellings subtract value because people mistype them.

### 4. TLD and extension risk

.com remains the default assumption for English-speaking buyers and the safest extension for offline marketing. A strong name in a weak extension usually loses to a good name in .com.

### 5. Brandability

Can the name be said out loud, spelled over a phone, and used as a logo without explanation? Brandable names compress marketing spend, which is why companies pay for them.

### 6. Scarcity and substitutability

The most under-appreciated input: **can the buyer get something close for less?** If three similar names are available, pricing power collapses. Exact-match names with no reasonable substitute behave like a one-of-one asset, because that is literally what a domain registration is — one owner, one record, one time.

## How the multiples work in practice

Broker guidance clusters around a few rules of thumb for domains in the aftermarket:

| Buyer type | Typical range |
| --- | --- |
| Investor-to-investor resale | 2×–5× the original acquisition cost |
| End user, tactical fit | Low four figures to mid five figures |
| Category-defining brand, no substitute | Mid five figures to six figures and beyond |

The jump between rows is where the money is. An investor buys for scarcity; an end user buys for revenue they expect the name to unlock.

## Putting it together: a worked example

Take an exact-match .com that combines two high-intent education words, is 18 characters with no hyphen, has a clean 10+ year registration history, and has no available alternative that says the same thing.

- Comparable sales of similar education .coms support a five-figure floor
- The words describe a real, funded market (corporate English training and language learning)
- Length and spelling make it usable in ads without qualification
- .com removes extension objections in procurement
- No substitute exists — the buyer either gets this name or rebrands

That combination is what supports a five-figure buy-it-now price rather than a "make an offer" listing. The seller is not pricing a web page; they are pricing a permanent brand asset.

## Where owners get it wrong

- **Pricing from ego.** The name you love is not the name the market values.
- **Ignoring holding costs.** Renewals, and opportunity cost, accrue every year a name sits unsold.
- **Refusing to compare.** Without comps, a price is a guess.
- **Forgetting the buyer's alternative.** Your BATNA is a year of branding around a worse name; theirs is usually "buy it or don't."

## Next steps

If you are pricing a name you own, start with comps and adjust for the six inputs above. If you are buying, price the name against the marketing budget it saves and the rebranding risk it removes.
