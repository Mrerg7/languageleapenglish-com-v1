Buying a domain from a stranger is a trust problem: one side has the money, the other has the asset, and neither wants to go first. Escrow solves it. Here is the process end to end, plus the failure modes to watch for.

## Step 1 — Establish who you are dealing with

Before money moves, confirm:

- The seller actually controls the domain (ask them to add a TXT record or sign a message with the registrar's contact address)
- The domain is not subject to a UDRP dispute or a pending transfer lock
- You are communicating on a channel you initiated, not one that arrived unsolicited

A seller who hesitates at verification is a seller to walk away from.

## Step 2 — Agree price and terms in writing

Get the price, the transfer method and the timeline in an email thread. Note whether the price includes:

- Escrow fees (usually split or paid by buyer)
- Any registrar push costs
- Whether the domain carries a website, content or mailing list — most sales are the registration only

If instalments are involved, put the schedule in the escrow agreement rather than in side-channel promises.

## Step 3 — Open an Escrow.com transaction

Escrow.com is the industry standard for domain sales and is licensed and regulated for this purpose. The flow is:

1. **Buyer funds escrow** — money leaves your account but is not released to the seller.
2. **Seller transfers the domain** — into escrow-controlled custody or to your registrar, depending on structure.
3. **Buyer inspects and accepts** — you confirm the domain is in your account and behaves as agreed.
4. **Seller is paid** — escrow releases funds.

Neither side can walk away with both the money and the asset.

## Step 4 — Choose your transfer method

Two standard mechanisms:

### Registrar push
The seller moves the domain to your account at the *same* registrar. Fastest option — often instant — and requires only your account ID at that registrar.

### Auth code (EPP)
The seller releases an authorization code; you start a transfer at your own registrar. Takes up to five days under ICANN rules, and the domain may be locked for 60 days if it recently changed hands or updated registrant contact details.

For most buyers, the push is simpler: you keep your existing registrar, and the domain appears in your account the same day.

## Step 5 — Verify after transfer

Once the domain is yours:

- Change the registrar account password and enable two-factor authentication
- Update the registrant email to an address you control
- Confirm the domain's nameservers and DNS are as you intend
- Lock the domain (registrar lock) so it cannot be moved without your action

Only then approve the escrow release.

## Common scams and how to avoid them

| Red flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| "Send crypto directly, it's faster" | Escrow exists precisely so nobody has to trust a stranger |
| A seller who won't verify control | They may not own the name |
| A marketplace link sent by email | Phishing domains imitate marketplaces to capture escrow payments |
| Pressure to release funds before DNS propagates | You may receive a name that is not yet usable |
| Prices far below comparables | Too-good-to-be-true pricing is a feature of fraud |

Many premium names are not on a marketplace at all — the owner lists the domain on its own page and handles enquiries directly. The checklist above applies identically — verify control, agree terms, fund escrow, transfer, inspect, release.

## After the acquisition

A domain is only the foundation. The next moves that matter:

- Point DNS at your host and confirm HTTPS works before announcing anything
- Reserve matching handles on the social platforms you intend to use
- Submit the new site to Google Search Console with a sitemap
- Set a renewal reminder and enable auto-renew at the registrar

Buy carefully, transfer through escrow, then build.
